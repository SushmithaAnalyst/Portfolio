What the Data Shows
First Class SLA is Broken
95.3%
Of 27,814 First Class orders are delivered late. The mode promises 1 day and averages 2. This is not an operations failure - the committed lead time was never achievable. Adjusting the SLA to reflect actual performance would immediately remove this from the late bucket.
Second Class: Promise vs Reality
76.6%
Late rate on 35,216 Second Class orders. Scheduled at 2 days, actual average is 3.99 days - nearly double. Every metric points to the same pattern: committed windows are set tighter than warehouse processing allows.
Same Day Still Misses Nearly Half
45.7%
Even Same Day - the fastest tier - misses its window 45.7% of the time. However it also shows the highest on-time rate at 49.7%, confirming the warehouse can process quickly. The inconsistency suggests scheduling, not capability, is the constraint.
Delays Are Systemic, Not Regional
0.85%
Spread in late delivery rates across all five markets. LATAM: 54.4%, Europe: 55.2%. The same pattern holds across customer segments (0.35 point spread) and product categories (0.5 point spread). The root cause is in the warehouse workflow, not the lane or product.
23% Capacity Is Being Used Early
41,592
Orders shipped ahead of the committed date (advance shipping). This is not a small buffer - it represents nearly a quarter of all volume. Redistributing this early-dispatch capacity toward the late-delivery pool is the most immediately actionable lever available.
Standard Class Is the Benchmark
38.1%
Standard Class has the lowest late rate because it commits to 4 days and averages exactly 4 days. It is the only mode where the SLA matches operational reality. This is the template: set lead times based on actual throughput, not aspirational targets.
